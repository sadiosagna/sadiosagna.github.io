Anonymous… or Not? The Ethics of Course Surveys in a Data-Driven Organization

SadioSagna | Nov 8, 2026 min read

In modern organizations, surveys are often viewed as a straightforward way to gather feedback and improve services. Educational institutions and corporate training departments regularly collect participant opinions to evaluate course quality, instructor effectiveness, and future training needs. However, what appears to be a simple survey can raise significant ethical concerns when examined through the lens of data science ethics.

Drawing on concepts from Martens (2022), Data Science Ethics: Concepts, Techniques, and Cautionary Tales, this blog post analyzes a course evaluation survey scenario in which participants complete an online survey after finishing a course. Although respondents are told that the survey is anonymous, they must access the survey through a web link and log in using organizational credentials. Executive management plans to use the results to determine which courses should be redesigned or updated. As the designated data analyst, it is important to recognize not only the value of the collected data but also the ethical implications surrounding its collection, storage, analysis, and use.

Participants complete a ten-question survey at the end of a course. To access the survey, they must:

• Visit a web-based survey link.

• Log in using their organizational username and password.

• Select the course being evaluated.

• Complete the survey.

The survey introduction states that responses will remain anonymous. However, because participants authenticate before responding, an immediate ethical question emerges: Can a survey truly be anonymous if users must identify themselves to gain access? Additionally, one of the survey questions asks respondents to rate a statement using a scale from “Strongly Agree” to “Strongly Disagree.” While seemingly harmless, the survey design reveals numerous ethical concerns when evaluated using data science ethics principles.

1. Ethical Issues Present in the Survey Design and Sample Question

A. Misleading Claims of Anonymity

The most obvious ethical concern is the survey’s claim that responses are anonymous while requiring authentication through organizational credentials. Even if the survey system is configured not to store usernames alongside responses, respondents may reasonably believe that their identity can still be linked to their answers. From an ethical standpoint, this creates a problem of transparency. According to ethical data practices, individuals must be informed accurately about how their data are collected and used. If respondents are led to believe that their identities cannot be connected to their responses when the system technically could allow such linkage, the survey may be considered misleading. This issue relates directly to:

• Informed consent

• Transparency

• Trustworthiness

• Data governance

When participants do not fully understand how their information is handled, their consent cannot be considered truly informed.

B. Potential Privacy Violations

Logging into the survey creates the possibility that technical data could identify respondents, including:

• Usernames

• Session identifiers

• Login logs

• IP addresses

• Device information

• Timestamp records

Even if these identifiers are not intentionally analyzed, their existence creates privacy risks.

In Martens’ framework, responsible data science requires minimizing unnecessary data collection. If organizational credentials are only needed to prevent duplicate submissions, alternative approaches may exist that collect less personally identifiable information.

This raises concerns regarding the ethical principle of privacy preservation.

C. Power Imbalance and Response Bias

Another ethical issue is the organizational relationship between respondents and management.

Employees or students may fear that negative responses could somehow be traced back to them. As a result, they may provide more favorable ratings than they actually believe.

This fear can undermine data quality by producing: • Inflated ratings

• Reduced criticism

• Lower survey honesty

• Distorted conclusions

An ethical survey should promote genuine participation without creating perceived risks for respondents.

D. Ambiguity in the Survey Question

Although the sample question uses a standard agreement scale, ethical concerns can arise if:

• The statement is vague.

• Key terms are not defined.

• Respondents interpret wording differently.

Data scientists have a responsibility to ensure that survey questions measure what they intend to measure. Poorly designed questions threaten the ethical principle of data quality because management may make important decisions based on unreliable or misunderstood responses.

E. Risk of Function Creep

Function creep occurs when data collected for one purpose are later used for another purpose without the participant’s original agreement.

Respondents are told that survey results will evaluate courses. However, if management later uses responses to evaluate instructors, departments, or specific participants, the original purpose changes.

This violates ethical expectations regarding:

• Purpose limitation

• Consent

• Fair use of data

2. Other Ethical Issues That Could Be Present

Beyond the issues explicitly described, several additional ethical risks may exist.

A. Data Security Risks

Survey data may contain sensitive comments or criticisms. If stored improperly, unauthorized individuals could gain access to identifiable information.

Questions include:

• Who can access survey data?

• How long is data retained?

• Is the information encrypted?

• Are there audit controls?

Ethical data stewardship requires strong safeguards to protect respondents.

B. Lack of Data Minimization

The survey may collect more information than necessary. Examples:

• Authentication logs

• Location data

• Browser information

• Demographic information

The principle of data minimization suggests collecting only what is necessary to achieve the stated objective.

C. Selection Bias

Only participants who complete the survey during the available time window can contribute responses. Potential consequences include:

• Underrepresentation of certain groups

• Overrepresentation of highly satisfied or dissatisfied individuals

• Distorted survey results

Biased samples lead to biased decisions, creating fairness concerns.

D. Nonresponse Bias

People with strong positive or negative opinions often participate at higher rates than individuals with neutral opinions.

If participation rates are low, executive management may receive an inaccurate picture of course quality.

Ethically, data analysts should disclose such limitations rather than present survey results as perfectly representative.

E. Re-identification Risk

Even if names are removed from survey responses, participants can sometimes be identified through combinations of information such as:

• Course attended

• Completion date

• Department

• Unique comments

This is known as re-identification risk, one of the most discussed issues in modern data ethics.

True anonymity is difficult to guarantee when datasets can be linked with other organizational records.

F. Lack of Accountability

An additional concern is whether the organization has established accountability mechanisms. Questions include:

• Who oversees survey ethics?

• Who validates anonymity claims?

• Who approves secondary data uses?

Without accountability structures, ethical promises may not be enforceable.

3. Recommendations to Executive Management

To address these ethical concerns, several improvements should be implemented.

Recommendation 1: Be Honest About Anonymity

If authentication occurs, the survey should not automatically be described as anonymous.

Instead, the organization should clearly explain:

• Why login is required

• What data are collected

• Whether usernames are stored

• How responses are separated from identities

Transparency builds trust and supports informed consent.

Recommendation 2: Adopt Privacy-by-Design Principles

Privacy should be built into the survey process from the beginning.

Possible measures include:

• Separating login records from survey responses

• Removing identifiers before analysis

• Encrypting stored data

• Restricting administrative access

These approaches reduce privacy risks while maintaining analytical usefulness.

Recommendation 3: Improve Consent Language

The survey introduction should clearly disclose:

• Survey objectives

• Data retention periods

• Access permissions

• Potential reporting uses

Participants should understand exactly how their information will be used.

Recommendation 4: Conduct an Ethical Risk Assessment

Before launching the survey, management should evaluate:

• Privacy risks

• Security vulnerabilities

• Re-identification risks

• Potential biases

This aligns with responsible data governance practices.

Recommendation 5: Improve Survey Design

Questions should be reviewed to ensure:

• Neutral wording

• Clear terminology

• Accessibility

• Lack of leading language

Higher-quality questions improve both ethics and decision-making.

Recommendation 6: Publish Aggregate Results Only

Reports should focus on aggregated outcomes rather than individual responses.

Examples include:

• Course-level averages

• Trend analysis

• Department-wide findings

Aggregation helps preserve anonymity while still providing valuable business insights.

Recommendation 7: Establish Governance and Accountability

Management should create formal policies governing:

• Data access

• Data retention

• Secondary use restrictions

• Compliance monitoring

Strong governance supports ethical data science throughout the survey lifecycle.

4. How Could a Data Analyst Proceed Ethically if Management Rejects These Recommendations?

Unfortunately, ethical conflicts sometimes arise when organizational leaders dismiss concerns.

If executive management insists that the survey is acceptable despite identified issues, several ethical options remain available.

A. Document Concerns Professionally

The analyst should create written documentation describing:

• Identified risks

• Ethical implications

• Recommended mitigations

This ensures transparency and demonstrates professional diligence.

B. Continue Advocating Through Evidence

Rather than framing objections as personal opinions, the analyst can present:

• Privacy principles

• Data governance standards

• Industry best practices

• Ethical frameworks from the literature

Evidence-based discussions are more likely to influence decision-makers.

C. Limit Misleading Claims

If reporting survey results, the analyst should avoid overstating conclusions.

For example:

• Acknowledge response limitations.

• Discuss possible biases.

• Clarify anonymity uncertainties.

Professional integrity requires honest communication.

D. Seek Guidance from Ethics or Compliance Officers

If available, concerns can be elevated to:

• Ethics committees

• Privacy officers

• Legal departments

• Compliance teams

This allows broader organizational review of identified risks.

E. Follow Professional Codes of Conduct

Data professionals have ethical responsibilities that extend beyond management preferences.

Principles such as:

• Honesty

• Fairness

• Accountability

• Respect for privacy

should guide decision-making.

F. Consider Whistleblower Protections if Necessary

In extreme situations involving serious legal or ethical violations, formal reporting channels may be appropriate. Such actions should follow organizational policies and applicable laws.

Conclusion

At first glance, this course evaluation survey appears to be a routine feedback mechanism. However, a deeper ethical analysis reveals substantial concerns regarding anonymity, privacy, transparency, data quality, bias, and accountability. The requirement that participants log in while simultaneously being told that responses are anonymous creates an especially important tension that may undermine both trust and data integrity.

Using concepts from Martens’ Data Science Ethics, we can identify several relevant ethical principles, including informed consent, transparency, privacy preservation, data minimization, accountability, fairness, data quality, governance, re-identification risk, and purpose limitation. These concepts demonstrate that ethical data science is not merely about collecting information but about ensuring that collection, analysis, and use of data respect the rights and expectations of the individuals providing it.

Ultimately, organizations obtain better insights when participants trust the process. Ethical survey design is therefore not only the right thing to do, but also a practical strategy for generating more reliable data, better decisions, and stronger organizational credibility.
