Beyond Compliance: Machine Learning in Pharmacovigilance and Drug Safety and the Limits of AI Regulation

SadioSagna | Nov 8, 2026 min read

Can artificial intelligence make medicines safer? Increasingly, the answer appears to be yes. Yet as machine learning transforms pharmacovigilance and drug safety, an important ethical question emerges: who governs the algorithms that help protect patients? While laws and regulations provide an essential foundation, they often struggle to keep pace with rapidly evolving AI technologies. This gap highlights the growing importance of soft ethics, a complementary approach that promotes responsible innovation beyond legal compliance.

Introduction: AI’s Expanding Role in Drug Safety

Artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) are revolutionizing pharmacovigilance, the science of detecting, assessing, understanding, and preventing adverse effects of medicines. Pharmaceutical companies, regulatory agencies, and healthcare organizations increasingly use ML algorithms to process large volumes of adverse event reports, identify safety signals, predict drug-related risks, and improve decision-making.

Organizations such as the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA), the European Medicines Agency (EMA), and major pharmaceutical companies are exploring AI-powered systems to enhance patient safety. Machine learning can analyze millions of records far faster than human reviewers, enabling earlier detection of potential safety concerns.

However, the deployment of AI in drug safety introduces significant ethical challenges. Regulatory frameworks often lag behind technological advancements, making it difficult to ensure that AI systems remain transparent, fair, accountable, and trustworthy. Data-driven technologies can create ethical risks that extend beyond legal requirements. This is where soft ethics becomes particularly valuable.

This blog explores the ethical challenges of machine learning in pharmacovigilance, examines the limitations of current AI regulation, and discusses how soft ethics can help address governance gaps.

Machine Learning Applications in Pharmacovigilance

Pharmacovigilance traditionally relies on human experts reviewing Individual Case Safety Reports (ICSRs), scientific literature, electronic health records, and post-marketing surveillance data. Machine learning expands these capabilities by automating many labor-intensive processes.

Common applications include:

1. Adverse Event Detection

Natural Language Processing (NLP) algorithms can extract adverse event information from physician notes, patient reports, social media posts, and medical literature.

2. Signal Detection

Machine learning models can identify unusual reporting patterns that may indicate previously unknown drug safety risks.

3. Case Prioritization

AI systems can classify and prioritize safety reports for human review, reducing workload and improving efficiency.

4. Predictive Risk Assessment

Algorithms can estimate which patient populations may be more vulnerable to adverse drug reactions based on demographic, clinical, and genetic factors.

These applications have tremendous potential to improve public health. However, greater capability also creates greater ethical responsibility.

The Limits of Regulation

Regulation remains a cornerstone of responsible AI deployment. Governments worldwide are developing AI governance frameworks to address risks associated with automated decision-making.

Despite these efforts, regulation faces several limitations.

Regulation Is Reactive Rather Than Proactive

Technology evolves much faster than legislation.

Machine learning models can be developed, deployed, and updated within months, whereas regulatory changes often take years. By the time lawmakers address one issue, new technologies may have introduced entirely different ethical concerns.

In pharmacovigilance, AI systems may continuously learn from incoming data. Regulators often struggle to evaluate dynamic systems that change over time.

Global Inconsistency

AI governance varies significantly across jurisdictions.

For example:

• The European Union has adopted comprehensive AI regulations emphasizing risk-based oversight.

• The United States generally relies on sector-specific regulations.

• Other countries remain in earlier stages of AI governance development.

Pharmaceutical companies operating globally may face inconsistent expectations regarding transparency, accountability, and privacy.

Difficulty Assessing Algorithmic Complexity

Many machine learning systems function as “black boxes.”

Deep learning models can generate predictions without providing easily understandable explanations. Regulators may find it difficult to determine:

• Why a particular safety signal was identified.

• Whether bias influenced outcomes.

• How errors might affect patient safety.

Traditional regulatory methods were not designed for highly complex, adaptive algorithms.

Compliance Does Not Guarantee Ethical Behavior

Perhaps the most important limitation is that legal compliance and ethical responsibility are not the same thing.

An AI system may fully comply with applicable regulations while still producing harmful outcomes due to bias, poor design decisions, or inadequate oversight.

This distinction reflects a major theme throughout Data Science Ethics: ethical responsibility must extend beyond simply following the law.

Key Ethical Risks in AI-Driven Pharmacovigilance

Several ethical concepts studied throughout the course are particularly relevant to AI applications in drug safety.

1. Bias and Fairness

Bias occurs when algorithms systematically disadvantage certain groups.

A machine learning model trained primarily on data from specific populations may perform poorly for underrepresented groups.

For example:

• Rare disease patients may be overlooked.

• Minority populations may be underrepresented in safety datasets.

• Algorithmic predictions may not reflect diverse patient experiences.

Fairness requires ensuring that AI systems produce equitable outcomes across populations.

2. Transparency

Transparency refers to the ability to understand how a system functions and reaches decisions.

In pharmacovigilance, regulators, healthcare providers, and pharmaceutical companies need confidence that AI-generated safety findings are reliable.

Without transparency, stakeholders may struggle to trust AI recommendations.

3. Accountability

When an AI system misses a serious adverse safety signal, who is responsible? Potentially:

• Software developers

• Pharmaceutical companies

• Regulatory agencies

• Safety reviewers

Accountability mechanisms become blurred when decision-making is partially automated.

Establishing clear responsibility remains a critical ethical requirement.

4. Privacy

Machine learning often relies on large datasets containing sensitive health information.

Ethical data science requires:

• Data minimization

• Secure storage

• Appropriate consent practices

• Protection against re-identification risks

Patient privacy remains fundamental to maintaining public trust.

5. Explainability

Explainability differs from transparency by focusing on the ability to communicate specific AI decisions in understandable terms.

Healthcare professionals are unlikely to trust recommendations they cannot interpret.

Explainable AI helps bridge the gap between algorithmic outputs and human judgment.

A Real-World Example: IBM Watson for Oncology

One widely discussed example illustrating the challenges of AI governance in healthcare is IBM Watson for Oncology.

Watson was developed to assist clinicians by recommending cancer treatment options using machine learning and medical knowledge databases.

Although the system generated significant excitement, reports later revealed concerns regarding reliability, transparency, and real-world performance. Critics argued that some recommendations were not sufficiently supported by clinical evidence and that decision-making processes were difficult to understand.

Importantly, many of these concerns were not necessarily violations of regulations. Rather, they highlighted broader ethical issues related to:

• Transparency

• Explainability

• Accountability

• Human oversight

The Watson experience demonstrates that regulatory compliance alone does not guarantee trustworthy AI.

It also illustrates why healthcare organizations must consider ethical principles throughout the AI lifecycle, from design and development to deployment and monitoring.

The Role of Soft Ethics

Soft ethics refers to ethical principles, professional standards, best practices, and voluntary governance mechanisms that operate beyond minimum legal requirements.

Rather than asking, “What are we legally allowed to do?” soft ethics asks, “What is the responsible thing to do?”

This approach aligns closely with the principles discussed throughout Data Science Ethics.

Soft Ethics as a Complement to Regulation

Soft ethics does not replace regulation.

Instead, it helps address areas where laws are incomplete, outdated, or insufficiently detailed.

Examples include:

• Professional codes of conduct

• Ethical review boards

• Responsible AI frameworks

• Industry best practices

• Organizational ethics committees

These mechanisms can evolve more quickly than legislation.

Encouraging Ethical-by-Design Development

Soft ethics promotes integrating ethical considerations during system design rather than after deployment.

For pharmacovigilance AI systems, organizations can:

• Conduct bias assessments

• Evaluate societal impacts

• Perform algorithmic audits

• Include multidisciplinary review teams

• Document ethical decision-making processes

This proactive approach reduces the likelihood of future harm.

Strengthening Trust

Trust is essential in healthcare and drug safety.

Patients must trust that their data are handled responsibly.

Healthcare professionals must trust AI recommendations.

Regulators must trust safety evaluations.

Soft ethics contributes to trustworthiness by emphasizing:

• Integrity

• Transparency

• Responsibility

• Respect for persons

These values support long-term acceptance of AI technologies.

Applying Data Science Ethics Principles to AI Systems

The ethical principles provide a practical framework for AI governance in pharmacovigilance.

Beneficence

AI systems should promote patient well-being and improve public health outcomes.

Non-Maleficence

Organizations should actively identify and minimize risks of harm caused by algorithmic errors or biased predictions.

Justice and Fairness

Models should be evaluated across diverse populations to prevent inequitable outcomes.

Respect for Autonomy

Patients should understand how their data are used and retain appropriate control over personal information.

Accountability

Clear governance structures must define responsibility for AI-enabled decisions.

Transparency and Explainability

Stakeholders should be able to understand how AI systems operate and how conclusions are reached.

Collectively, these principles create a foundation for ethical AI that extends beyond technical performance measures.
